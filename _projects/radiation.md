Radiation injures the lung in two connected settings. Patients receiving radiotherapy for lung cancer are limited by the radiosensitivity of normal lung tissue, which restricts the dose that can be safely delivered to a tumor. Survivors of a radiological or nuclear incident face delayed effects of acute radiation exposure, a slow progression of airway injury and scarring for which no medical countermeasure is currently approved in the lung. In both cases the conducting airways are central. They are where inhaled radioactive particles deposit and where epithelial injury, failed mucus clearance, and fibrotic remodeling drive long term breathing problems. Current understanding relies on animal models that respond differently from humans and on cell lines that do not reproduce normal airway biology.

To close this gap, we use our organ tissue equivalent (OTE) technology, miniature lab grown human tissues that reproduce the structure and function of real organs. Our airway OTE forms a differentiated, mucus-producing, ciliated epithelium at an air-liquid interface on a natural extracellular matrix. It stays functional for months and can be sampled repeatedly, so a single construct yields a full time course of injury rather than a single snapshot.

We expose these tissues to clinically relevant X-ray doses and follow both acute and delayed effects. DNA double-strand breaks appear within hours, followed over weeks by loss of ciliary activity, breakdown of the epithelial architecture, and collagen deposition that marks fibrosis. Because a countermeasure's benefit depends on timing, we are mapping the window after exposure during which the fibrotic outcome can still be changed, and testing whether cellular senescence and macrophage recruitment commit the tissue to permanent scarring.

To extend the platform to cancer therapy, we combine human lung cancer cells with healthy airway tissue in one construct, allowing side by side comparison of tumor and normal tissue radiosensitivity.

This work establishes a human, physiologically relevant platform for protecting the lung from radiation injury and improving cancer radiotherapy, while reducing reliance on animal testing.
