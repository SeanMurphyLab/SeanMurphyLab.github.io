More than two million U.S. military personnel deployed to war zones over the past two decades, and many encountered hazardous airborne substances during service. Two exposures stand out. The first is smoke from burn pits, which were widely used to dispose of waste on military bases. The second is vapor from military jet fuels. Both have been linked to long term lung and other health effects in Veterans, and concern about jet fuel contributed to passage of the PACT Act, which mandates investigation of its long term health impacts. Through two programs funded by the Department of Defense Toxic Exposures Research Program (TERP), we are applying our organ tissue equivalent (OTE) technology, miniature lab grown human tissues that reproduce the function and responses of real organs, to understand these exposures and to develop ways to protect those affected.

The first program investigates burn pit smoke. Different fuels, such as plywood, cardboard, and plastic, and different burning conditions produce smoke that varies widely in toxicity. Working with collaborators who generate smoke condensates representing real military burn pit conditions, we expose airway OTEs to each smoke type, characterize how harmful it is, and identify the specific chemicals most likely to cause lung injury and disease.

The second program examines jet fuel. It combines our OTE models with animal exposure studies from the Air Force Research Laboratory and samples from Service Members with documented exposure, provided by the Department of Veterans Affairs. We expose lung, liver, and brain OTEs to JP-5 and JP-8 jet fuels and track how the fuels are absorbed, distributed, and cleared. Comparing molecular signatures across the laboratory, animal, and human samples allows us to identify shared mechanisms and biomarkers of jet fuel toxicity.

Together, these programs aim to deliver diagnostic tools that identify disease risk in exposed Service Members, guidance to prevent harmful exposures in the future, and candidate FDA approved drugs that could be repurposed to treat exposure related disease. Because the work is laboratory based, it offers a low risk path to new data and treatments while reducing reliance on animal testing.
