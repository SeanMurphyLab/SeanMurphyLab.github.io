Microplastics have become one of the most widespread environmental exposures of modern life, and recent attention has focused on the microplastics we inhale at home and in our vehicles. In these environments, the majority of airborne particles small enough to reach the lungs are fibers between one and ten micrometers long, dominated by polyethylene and polyamide (nylon), the same plastics found in clothing, packaging, bottles, and everyday household goods. Yet most laboratory studies of inhaled microplastics rely on polystyrene spheres, a convenient commercial product that poorly reflects the shape and composition of what people actually breathe. As a result, the respiratory health consequences of realistic microplastic exposure remain largely unknown.

Our work asks whether inhaled polyethylene and polyamide fibers damage the airways, impair lung function, and drive the scarring process known as fibrosis. Central to these questions are alveolar macrophages, the resident immune cells that serve as sentinels of the lung and are the first to encounter inhaled particles. Earlier studies have shown that certain inhaled substances can leave lasting epigenetic marks on these cells, shifting them toward a chronic inflammatory state that promotes tissue injury long after an exposure ends. Whether common household plastics act in the same way is a key open question.

To address it, we combine complementary models rather than relying on any single system. Animal respiratory exposure studies capture whole-organ physiology and the progression of disease, while our human airway organ tissue equivalents provide a controlled, human-relevant platform for measuring cellular, functional, and molecular responses. Integrating data across animal and human systems strengthens the biological signal and helps separate genuine disease pathways from the artifacts of any one model. Together, this approach aims to establish causal links between everyday microplastic exposure and respiratory disease, and to reveal the mechanisms that connect them.
