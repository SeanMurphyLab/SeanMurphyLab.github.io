New Approach Methodologies, or NAMs, are testing systems that predict human biological responses without relying on animals. The NIH Complement-ARIE program supports the development, validation, and adoption of these methods to fill the most pressing gaps in biomedical research, with an emphasis on biological complexity, high-throughput screening, combining complementary approaches, and open data sharing.

Our contribution focuses on fibrosis, the progressive tissue scarring that drives diseases such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and the lung damage that can follow viral infection or environmental exposure. Only two anti-fibrotic drugs have FDA approval, and neither reverses established scarring. Several recent candidates that succeeded in animal studies later failed in large human trials, showing how poorly animal models predict outcomes in people.

We are building a human tissue platform that screens candidate drugs in two complementary tiers. The first tier uses a 3D bioprinting system to produce large numbers of reproducible fibrotic tissue models in a high-throughput format, allowing us to rank many compounds quickly. The second tier uses advanced airway organ tissue equivalents developed at WFIRM that recreate the biological complexity of human airway tissue and its progressive scarring, with quantifiable endpoints such as tissue stiffness, matrix composition, and clinically relevant biomarkers.

Consistent with the program's goals, the platform captures fibrotic mechanisms shared across diseases, generates structured data suited to shared repositories, and is designed for independent validation. We benchmark against healthy and fibrotic human lung tissue and test established reference drugs alongside past clinical failures, and we expect to reduce animal use in early development by an estimated 80 to 90 percent.

The project unites WFIRM's model-building experience, an industry partner, regulatory science expertise, and clinical collaboration through Advocate Health, working toward recognition of the platform as a qualified drug development tool.

For more information, see the [NIH Complement-ARIE program](https://commonfund.nih.gov/complementarie).
